01

Know what you’re looking at.

Use the official ballot as your checklist. Use this guide as an explanation—not a recommendation.

  1. Confirm your ballot

    Find your precinct and sample ballot through the Broward Supervisor of Elections. District boundaries, not ZIP codes, determine which races you see.

  2. Separate facts from advocacy

    A party label identifies affiliation. An endorsement identifies a supporter. Neither, by itself, explains how a candidate would govern.

  3. Read “yes” and “no” literally

    For each measure, compare the proposed change with what happens if it does not pass. Check the full official text when a summary leaves you uncertain.

03

Ballot measures, in plain English

The change being proposed, the effect of a yes or no vote, and who formally put it on the ballot.

State amendments need at least 60% approval. These explanations are paraphrases; the linked official wording controls. County and school questions are distinct from state amendments.

Florida · Amendment 1

A larger state rainy-day fund

Changes how Florida builds and uses its Budget Stabilization Fund.

Yes

Raises the reserve ceiling from 10% to 25% of general revenue. Generally requires annual transfers of the lesser of $750 million or the amount needed to reach the new ceiling. Adds a critical-need withdrawal route.

No

Keeps the current constitutional framework.

What to know: Moves state revenue into reserves; it is not a new household tax. Critical-need exceptions have legislative supermajority and balance safeguards. Effective January 5, 2027.

Origin: Legislative referral, HJR 5019; sponsor Lawrence McClure (Republican). House: 100–1. Senate: 29–4.

Sources: Senate explanation · Sponsor & votes · Official ballot wording

Florida · Amendment 2

Tax exemption for qualifying farm equipment

Creates a value-based property-tax exemption for certain tangible personal property used in agriculture or agritourism.

Yes

Exempts qualifying movable property regularly kept on agricultural-classified land, owned by the landowner or leaseholder and used for agricultural production or agritourism, beginning in 2027.

No

Does not create this exemption.

What to know: This is not a blanket exemption for farmland, buildings or everyone’s household property. “Ad valorem” means based on value.

Origin: Legislative referral, CS/HJR 1215; State Affairs Committee / Danny Alvarez (Republican). House: 110–1. Senate: 37–0.

Sources: Legislation, sponsor & votes · Official ballot wording

Florida · Amendment 3

Homestead exemptions & property-tax rules

A package of changes to non-school property taxes, assessment limits and local-government tax powers.

Yes

Raises the non-school homestead exemption to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 in 2028, then adjusts it for inflation. Lowers the annual non-homestead assessment-growth cap from 10% to 5%. Also changes local exemption and spending rules.

No

Keeps existing constitutional provisions rather than adopting this package.

What to know: School taxes are excluded. An exemption reduces taxable value—it is not a cash payment. A 5% assessment cap does not cap the tax bill. New residents generally wait until their fifth exemption year for the larger amount. The spending provisions allow additional approved expenditures; they are not an absolute ban on all unlisted spending. Actual savings and service effects depend on later decisions and property-specific facts.

Origin: Legislative referral, CS/HJR 1-F; State Affairs Committee / Toby Overdorf (Republican). House: 75–26. Senate: 30–9.

Sources: Senate explanation · Sponsor & votes · Official ballot wording

Broward · County question 1

A quarter-percent health-care sales surtax

Adds a temporary sales tax for county health services.

For

Approves an additional 0.25% sales surtax for five years, funding eligible uninsured heart/cancer screening, primary and preventive care for indigent or medically poor residents, and other services in the county health plan.

Against

Rejects this new tax and funding mechanism—not all existing health services.

What to know: 0.25% means 25 cents per $100 of taxable purchases. The surtax ends automatically if a 1% infrastructure surtax is levied; this question does not enact that separate tax. The printed choices are FOR / AGAINST THE 0.25 CENT TAX.

Origin: Broward County ballot referral; final Resolution 2026-114.

Sources: Final adopted resolution · County ballot overview

Broward · County question 2

An alternative route for plat approval

A plat is a recorded map dividing land. This changes who may approve one.

Yes

Allows the county commission to adopt an alternative process by ordinance, including staff approval where state law allows.

No

Retains the charter requirement for commission approval before recording.

What to know: Permits a later ordinance; it does not automatically approve a development or repeal zoning and building rules.

Origin: Broward County charter amendment; Resolution 2026-091.

Sources: Final adopted resolution

Broward · County question 3

Selecting the county auditor

Changes the independent nominating board’s shortlist, not whether the auditor is elected.

Yes

Has the nominating board propose three qualified candidates; the commission selects one by majority vote.

No

Keeps the current process: the board nominates one candidate for commission ratification.

What to know: The independent nominating board remains. Voters do not directly elect the auditor under either option.

Origin: Broward County charter amendment.

Sources: Final adopted resolution

Broward · School Board question 1

Renewing the one-mill school levy

Continues an existing property-tax levy for school needs from January 1, 2027 through December 31, 2030.

Yes

Renews funding for school safety, mental-health services, teacher pay and school-based support staff in public and charter schools.

No

Does not renew this levy; it does not eliminate all school property taxes.

What to know: One mill equals $1 for each $1,000 of taxable value: $300 per year on $300,000 taxable value, as an arithmetic example—not your bill. State Amendment 3’s larger exemption applies to non-school taxes.

Origin: School Board referendum.

Sources: School Board’s official wording

Fort Lauderdale charter questions

Ten separate decisions about the city’s governing rules. Each needs a majority, not the 60% required for state constitutional amendments. A yes adopts that question’s change; a no rejects it.

Fort Lauderdale · Question 1

Qualifications, organizational meeting, vice mayor

Yes

Revises candidate qualifications, including twelve months' continuous residency before qualifying (with a redistricting exception for commissioner candidates), instead of six months; requires city-elector status and residency documentation. Seats elected officials at the first regular commission meeting after election certification and adjusts when the vice mayor is designated.

No

Keeps the existing qualifications and organizational timetable.

What to know: This is a bundled eligibility-and-procedure change, not merely a change to meeting dates. It governs candidates, not voter-registration eligibility.

Origin: City commission charter referral; local governing rules, not a citizen petition or a state amendment.

Source: Official city ordinance and ballot wording.

Fort Lauderdale · Question 2

Submission of candidate documentation

Yes

Expressly requires mayoral/commission candidates to submit compliance documents to the city clerk for a ministerial review.

No

Rejects that new charter procedure.

What to know: Ministerial review means document-processing/checking, not giving the clerk the court's role deciding legal challenges.

Origin: City commission charter referral; local governing rules, not a citizen petition or a state amendment.

Source: Official city ordinance and ballot wording.

Fort Lauderdale · Question 3

Candidate challenges

Yes

Removes the city commission's authority to receive voter affidavits alleging a candidate is unqualified or violated the charter and to decide those allegations; leaves those disputes to courts.

No

Retains the commission's charter role.

What to know: It changes who considers challenges, not whether candidates must qualify or can be challenged in court.

Origin: City commission charter referral; local governing rules, not a citizen petition or a state amendment.

Source: Official city ordinance and ballot wording.

Fort Lauderdale · Question 4

Special meetings

Yes

Requires at least 72 hours' notice for ordinary special commission meetings; permits whatever notice is practicable during an emergency involving potential imminent harm or averting a catastrophe.

No

Retains current charter notice provisions.

What to know: The 72-hour rule has an explicit emergency exception. It is not a promise that every meeting gets three days' notice.

Origin: City commission charter referral; local governing rules, not a citizen petition or a state amendment.

Source: Official city ordinance and ballot wording.

Fort Lauderdale · Question 5

Civil Service Board

Yes

Changes the employee-nomination process: the five employees with most nominations form the nominating committee, ties use continuous city seniority, and it proposes up to three candidates. Employee voting still selects the recommended board appointee. Two nominating-committee members replace two commissioners on the election-certification board. Removes the Civil Service Board's job-classification-specification review/advisory role.

No

Keeps existing nomination/election procedures and job-classification review role.

What to know: The board is not abolished. Employee appeals and personnel-rule review remain in the ordinance.

Origin: City commission charter referral; local governing rules, not a citizen petition or a state amendment.

Source: Official city ordinance and ballot wording.

Fort Lauderdale · Question 6

Primary references, notices, manager titles

Yes

Deletes primary-election references, removes the charter's requirement for two newspaper publications for public hearings and other notices, and distinguishes an interim manager after resignation/termination from an acting manager during temporary absence.

No

Keeps those existing charter provisions.

What to know: The package includes a substantive notice-publication change alongside terminology cleanup. It does not itself say that all public-notice obligations disappear.

Origin: City commission charter referral; local governing rules, not a citizen petition or a state amendment.

Source: Official city ordinance and ballot wording.

Fort Lauderdale · Question 7

City property transactions

Yes

Rewrites city property sale/lease procedures. Certain property transfers require four commissioner votes and a city-best-interest finding. For private use agreements including renewals/extensions: over 1–3 years requires four votes; over 3–10 years also requires independent fair-market-value and counterparty-qualification review; over 10 years also requires a business-viability report. Agreements over 50 years may be rejected through a referendum initiated by petitions from 15% of registered electors.

No

Keeps current charter transaction rules rather than this replacement framework.

What to know: This is not approval of a named project. The new rules replace existing detailed procedures; the package changes existing safeguards rather than only adding new ones. Park-specific restrictions are separately addressed in Question 8. The current 15% petition referendum provision applies to leases generally; the replacement limits that specific route to agreements longer than 50 years.

Origin: City commission charter referral; local governing rules, not a citizen petition or a state amendment.

Source: Official city ordinance and ballot wording.

Fort Lauderdale · Question 8

City-owned park land

Yes

Requires a unanimous vote of the entire commission for sale/transfer or leases over one year of land zoned Parks, Recreation and Open Space; covers other use agreements exceeding one year too. Unanimity also required to remove that zoning designation.

No

Retains current park-property protections rather than revised language.

What to know: This is a commission unanimity requirement, not a voter referendum requirement or an absolute prohibition. Existing charter's park-rezoning unanimity text referred to land zoned park on November 10, 2004; proposed wording covers land with the specified zoning without that date limitation.

Origin: City commission charter referral; local governing rules, not a citizen petition or a state amendment.

Source: Official city ordinance and ballot wording.

Fort Lauderdale · Question 9

Police chief

Yes

Makes Police Chief a charter position reporting directly to the city commission instead of the city manager; commission sets employment/compensation terms.

No

Leaves the existing manager-reporting arrangement.

What to know: Creates a charter-protected organizational role, not a new elected police chief and not a new police department.

Origin: City commission charter referral; local governing rules, not a citizen petition or a state amendment.

Source: Official city ordinance and ballot wording.

Fort Lauderdale · Question 10

Fire chief

Yes

Makes Fire Chief a charter position reporting directly to the city commission instead of the city manager; commission sets employment/compensation terms.

No

Leaves the existing manager-reporting arrangement.

What to know: Changes supervision and charter status; does not elect the chief or create a new fire department.

Origin: City commission charter referral; local governing rules, not a citizen petition or a state amendment.

Source: Official city ordinance and ballot wording.

How to read this guide

Label legend: Party and sponsor labels report documented facts, not inferred ideology. “Nonpartisan ballot” does not mean politically neutral. Absence of a documented endorsement is not proof of neutrality. All party labels receive equal visual treatment.

Official election records establish ballot wording, candidacy and applicable contests. Campaign or organization material can document that source’s own positions or endorsements; it is labeled accordingly, not treated as independent proof. “Sponsor” and “endorser” are different roles. Missing support information does not mean no endorsements exist. No candidate or voting choice is ranked or recommended.
